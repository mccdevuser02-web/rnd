01 · The Basics

What, exactly, is coffee?

At its core, coffee is simple: a fruit, roasted and ground, then coaxed by hot water. Everything else — altitude, variety, processing, roast, brew — is a refinement of that single act.

Coffee is a beverage made from roasted beans — the seeds of a small red fruit that grows on the evergreen Coffea shrub. The shrub itself belongs to the madder family, the same botanical family that gives us gardenia and the cinchona tree that yields quinine. When the fruit ripens, it turns from green to a deep cherry red, and the seeds inside are ready to be harvested.

Like wine, coffee is shaped by place. The “bean belt” — the tropical band between the Tropic of Cancer and the Tropic of Capricorn — is the only place coffee grows at scale: it needs consistent warmth, steady rainfall, and the cool nights that come with altitude. The higher a coffee is grown, the slower it ripens, and the denser and more complex the bean tends to be.

That is why a coffee from a volcanic highland in Ethiopia tastes nothing like one from a lowland farm in Vietnam — and why, once you understand the chain from cherry to cup, every variable you change leaves a fingerprint in the glass.

02 · The Story

A journey through five centuries

From highland goats to global industry — a short history of the drink that rewired the way the world wakes up, thinks, and talks.

  1. 15th c.

    Awakening in the highlands

    Ethiopian legend credits a goat herder named Kaldi with noticing his goats dancing after eating certain berries. Whether or not the story is true, by the 1400s Sufi mystics in Yemen were brewing coffee to stay awake for night prayers.

  2. 1554

    The first coffee houses

    Istanbul's qahveh khaneh opens its doors: coffee brewed unfiltered in a copper ibrik, served with conversation, chess and music. “Turkish coffee,” the oldest surviving method, still tastes of this era.

  3. 1600s

    A bitter potion in Europe

    Venetian merchants carry coffee to Venice; London follows in the 1650s and Paris in the 1680s. Critics call it “the bitter invention of Arabia,” but the salons — and the coffee houses that become engines of the Enlightenment — adopt it anyway.

  4. 1720s

    One seedling changes the world

    A young plant carried from Paris's Jardin des Plantes to Martinique becomes the mother of nearly every coffee tree in Brazil. A single tree's descendants now supply roughly a third of the world's coffee.

  5. 1880s

    Brazil rises

    Plantations spread across South America, and coffee exports begin to fund a nation's industrialization. Brazil becomes — and remains — the world's largest producer.

  6. 1903

    Espresso, born in Milan

    Luigi Bezzera patents the first pressure espresso machine, compressing an hour of brewing into a thirty-second shot. Italy's bars will perfect the rest.

  7. 1971–now

    The third wave

    From a single shop in Berkeley to specialty bars on every continent, coffee is treated with the reverence once reserved for wine: single origins, precise brews, and a conversation about flavor.

03 · The Bean

Two species, three paths

Nearly every cup on Earth comes from one of two species — and every one of them travels one of three processing paths before it ever meets heat.

Arabica

Coffea arabica — the connoisseur's bean
Grown at
600–2,200 m, in cool, often volcanic highlands
Caffeine
~1.2% — gentle by design
Temperament
Delicate, slow-growing, sensitive to rust and heat

Curious fact

Robusta

~40% of production
Coffea canephora — the workhorse
Grown at
0–800 m, in hot, low, unforgiving land
Caffeine
~2.2% — nearly double Arabica, a natural pesticide
Temperament
Hardy, fast-growing, shrugs off disease and drought
Flavor
Bold, bitter, earthy, chocolatey; a dense, lasting crema
Curious fact
The backbone of most espresso blends and virtually all instant coffee

Why it matters. The species is the first — and the largest — decision in any cup. Blenders layer Robusta into espresso for body and crema; purists reserve Arabica for single-origin cups. In practice, the species sets the ceiling, and everything downstream refines it.

04 · The Roast

Where green meets golden

Green coffee is grassy and faintly vegetal. Roasting is where hundreds of flavor compounds are born — and where the single biggest variable in your cup takes control.

Light Medium Dark
First crack

Light

“It tastes of where it grew.”

Just past the first audible crack in the bean. Maximum acidity, delicate florals, tea-like body. The origin's fingerprint is preserved almost intact.

Caramelization

Medium

“It tastes of balance.”

Sugars caramelize, body builds, acidity rounds off. Chocolate, nuts, caramel. The most common roast in the world — the default that keeps everyone comfortable.

Second crack

Dark

“It tastes of the roast.”

Past the second crack, oils migrate to the surface and origin notes give way to smoke, bittersweet char and a heavy body. Bold, unmistakable, and — to purists — a different drink.

The chemistry, briefly. Between roughly 150 °C and 200 °C, the Maillard reaction and caramelization turn simple sugars and amino acids into hundreds of new aromatics, while roasting drives out moisture until the bean holds about 1–2%. The first crack is steam and structure expanding; the second is the cell walls themselves splitting.

06 · The Finer Details

The science of the golden cup

A cup of coffee is a controlled experiment you run for yourself. Here are the numbers the professionals keep in their heads.

1,000+aroma compounds identified in a single cup
18–22%of the bean's mass dissolves in an ideal brew
98.5%of your cup is water — treat it like an ingredient
90–96°the extraction window for most filter brews

The five dials

Dose & yield

The ratio sets strength: roughly 1:2 for espresso, 1:15–1:17 for filter. Fix this first — it changes everything.

Grind

Particle size sets the speed of extraction. Too fine — bitter, slow. Too coarse — sour, fast. Change one thing at a time.

Temperature

Hotter water extracts faster. Below ~85 °C you lose sweetness; above ~96 °C you risk scorching the good notes.

Time

Time is the result of the other dials. With a consistent grind, a brew that runs long or short is your diagnostic.

Water

75–150 ppm of dissolved minerals, no chlorine. Hard water under-extracts; ultra-soft water tastes hollow. Bottled mineral water is a legitimate upgrade.

Freshness — the silent variable

A roasted bean releases CO₂ for weeks, and a ground one surrenders most of its aroma within ten to fifteen minutes. Give a roast 7–21 days to settle, store it airtight away from light, and grind immediately before brewing. The difference between a bag opened this morning and one opened this week is the difference between a cup that tastes of a place and one that merely tastes like coffee.

07 · The Cup

Reading flavor like a professional

Cupping — the ritual of tasting raw, unroasted-of-context coffee side by side — is how the industry names what it tastes of. It takes six steps, and a loud slurp.

  1. Sniff the dry grounds

    Aroma before a drop of water: the first hint of what the origin will say.

  2. Bloom & wait

    Add hot water, wait four minutes. The grounds swell and a “crust” forms on top.

  3. Break the crust

    Stir and smell again — this is the most information-rich moment of the entire cupping.

  4. Skim

    Lift away the foam and sludge so you taste the liquid, not the grounds.

  5. Slurp, loudly

    Aerating the coffee spreads it across the whole palate — and sends aroma straight up through the nose.

  6. Score it

    Evaluate aroma, flavor, aftertaste, body, acidity and balance on a 100-point scale.
